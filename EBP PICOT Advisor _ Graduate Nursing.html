Step 2 of 10

Identifying Your Clinical Problem

This step uses three guided stages adapted from Lean Six Sigma DMAIC principles to help you arrive at a single, specific, measurable clinical problem. You will move through them one at a time. A running summary of your answers stays visible so you always know where you are.

Why start with the problem, not the solution? The most common graduate student mistake is choosing an intervention before understanding the problem. Lean Six Sigma DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control) is one of the most widely validated QI frameworks in healthcare. It insists on a clear, data-supported problem before any solution is considered. These three stages apply that discipline in a format designed for nursing EBP and QI projects.
Important reminder for DNP students. Your project must be a practice change or QI initiative, not a research study. If your problem statement sounds like a research question, reframe it as a practice gap that an evidence-based intervention can address. Research requiring full IRB review is not compatible with practicum timelines.

Stage 1Describe the Problem with Data
In Lean Six Sigma, the Define and Measure phases require you to state what is happening in neutral, observable, measurable terms -- no causes, no solutions. Just what you see and how you can count it.
Two things that are NOT clinical problems: "Nurses lack knowledge about X" is not a problem statement -- it is already a conclusion about a cause. "I want to survey patients about anxiety" is not a problem -- it is a research idea. Start with what is actually happening to patients or to care quality that you can observe or measure.

Weak vs. strong: Weak: "patients on my unit." Strong: "adult patients aged 65 and older with two or more fall risk factors admitted to a medical-surgical unit."
Weak vs. strong: Weak: "nurses need education about falls." Strong: "our unit's fall rate was 3.2 per 1,000 patient days last quarter, compared to the national benchmark of 1.9."

Fishbone Diagram (Ishikawa) -- another Lean Six Sigma tool. A Fishbone Diagram organizes possible causes by category. Fill in whatever applies to your situation. Focus on system and process factors, not individual blame.
Lean Six Sigma framing: Avoid: "nurses don't know how to do X." Instead: "there is no standardized trigger or cue in the workflow that prompts nurses to reassess at the right time."
This must be a process or system issue, not a person. "Nurses don't know" is not a root cause -- it is a symptom. What process is missing or broken that would prevent this even if all nurses were fully trained?
Caution: Your root cause appears to identify a knowledge deficit. Identifying "lack of knowledge" as a root cause almost always leads students to propose a simple education session as the intervention -- which is the weakest possible solution and the least likely to produce a sustained change. Before accepting this as your root cause, ask yourself:

Knowledge about what, specifically? What would a nurse who "knows" do differently? If they knew, what would change in the process? If the answer is "they would follow the protocol," then the real root cause is the absence of a reliable protocol, not the absence of knowledge. Knowledge is a precondition; a working system is the solution.

Consider the Hierarchy of Controls (see Stage 3) -- fixing the system is always more effective and more sustainable than training people to work around a broken one.

Hierarchy of Controls (Lean Six Sigma / NIOSH framework). This framework ranks types of solutions by effectiveness. Higher-level controls are more reliable because they do not depend on individual human behavior. Click the level that best matches the type of intervention your root cause points toward. This does not commit you to a specific intervention -- you must still search the literature before deciding that.
Which level best describes the type of solution your root cause calls for?
1
Eliminate the hazard or problem entirely
Remove the condition that causes the problem. Most reliable because it does not depend on human behavior.
Example: Remove an unsafe step from a procedure, replace a device that causes injury, eliminate a redundant handoff that introduces error.
2
Substitute a safer or more reliable process
Replace a risky or ineffective process with one that is safer or more reliably performed.
Example: Replace verbal handoffs with a structured written tool, switch from an unvalidated screening form to a validated instrument.
3
Engineering or structural control
Build a safeguard into the system or environment so the safe action is easier than the unsafe one.
Example: Embed a mandatory reassessment trigger in the EHR, install bed alarm systems, redesign the workflow so the protocol step cannot be skipped.
4
Administrative or workflow control
Implement a standardized protocol, checklist, or policy that guides behavior. Effective but depends on consistent adherence.
Example: Implement a validated fall prevention bundle, introduce a structured SBAR handoff protocol, establish a nurse-driven catheter removal policy.
5
Training or behavioral reminder
Educate staff or provide reminders to change individual behavior. The least reliable control because it depends entirely on every individual consistently applying what they learned.
Example: Provide a one-time education session on fall prevention, give nurses a laminated reminder card, send an email about a policy.
A Pareto chart or stratification by unit, shift, or patient subgroup -- standard Lean Six Sigma tools -- helps identify where to focus. Narrowing your scope makes a practicum-length project feasible.
Within an 8 to 10 week window, measuring a process or implementation outcome is often more realistic than a full clinical outcome. The literature must still support a connection between your process change and the clinical result.
Be specific about the number, the direction of change, and how you will measure it. This must directly reflect the problem you identified in Stage 1.

Step 4 of 10

Is This Nursing-Sensitive?

A nursing-sensitive project is one where a nursing-led practice change, protocol, assessment tool, or care bundle can meaningfully affect the outcome without requiring physician orders, prescriptive authority, or FDA oversight.

Examples of strong nursing-sensitive topics
  • Fall prevention protocol or assessment bundle implementation
  • Pressure injury prevention care bundles
  • CAUTI or CLABSI prevention bundle adherence
  • Pain reassessment documentation compliance
  • Hand hygiene compliance improvement
  • Early mobility protocols in post-surgical patients
  • Nurse-led delirium, malnutrition, or depression screening tool implementation
  • Structured handoff communication tool adoption
  • Transitions of care communication improvement
  • Implementation of a validated clinical screening or assessment tool
Important: Using validated tools in EBP and QI projects. If your intervention involves implementing a screening tool, assessment instrument, or measurement tool, that tool must be validated for your population and setting. Validation means the tool has been tested for reliability and validity in peer-reviewed research. Using an unvalidated tool, such as one you or a colleague creates, changes your project from EBP or QI into research, which requires full IRB review and is not eligible for this course. Additionally, if you use a validated tool, you must not modify it without the original author's written permission. Modifying a validated tool also invalidates its psychometric properties and changes your project into research.

Does your project involve any of the following?

This topic falls outside the scope of a nursing-sensitive EBP project. Projects requiring prescribing authority or FDA-regulated product testing are not appropriate for this course. Please refocus on a change that nurses can implement independently.

Step 6 of 10

A Common Pitfall: The Education-Only Project

Read this section carefully before continuing. It addresses one of the most common missteps at the graduate level.

Why a simple pre/post knowledge test is not a strong graduate-level EBP project A one-time education session with a pre/post knowledge quiz is one of the most overused and least rigorous project designs at both the MSN and DNP level. Here is why it rarely holds up:
  • Knowledge gain does not equal behavior change or improved clinical outcomes.
  • A single session rarely produces a sustained, measurable difference in the outcome that matters.
  • These projects often lack a meaningful comparison and produce results that are not clinically significant.
  • There is rarely strong literature supporting a one-time education session as a standalone intervention for most outcomes.
  • At the DNP level, your project chair will expect systems-level thinking and a robust, evidence-guided design, not a quiz score.
What to consider instead Strong projects at this level implement an evidence-based protocol, care bundle, validated assessment tool, or structured process change embedded into daily practice. Examples:
  • Implementing a validated fall risk reassessment protocol on a specific unit
  • Introducing a structured handoff communication tool into a defined workflow
  • Implementing a nurse-driven delirium screening tool with a documented response algorithm
  • Introducing a structured early mobility protocol with defined triggers and documentation
  • Implementing a skin assessment bundle with defined reassessment intervals and accountability measures
When education can be an appropriate component If the literature supports a structured, multi-component educational intervention tied directly to a measurable clinical outcome, that is different from a simple quiz. The critical distinction is this: the expected outcome must be a reported, sustained improvement in a clinical result, such as a reduction in fall rate, improvement in correct medication administration, or reduction in hospital-acquired infection rate. An improvement in knowledge scores alone is not an acceptable primary outcome at the graduate level. The literature must drive the design, and the design must be built around a clinical outcome, not a knowledge test. You cannot decide on education and then look for literature to justify it.
A reminder about literature and evidence Whether your project is EBP or quality improvement, high-quality literature is not optional. The purpose of searching the evidence is to find an intervention that is already supported, not to justify an idea you have already decided on. This applies equally to MSN students writing a proposal and DNP students implementing a project. Your faculty or project chair will expect you to demonstrate that the intervention you chose emerged from the evidence, not the other way around.

With this in mind, how are you currently thinking about your project?

Please reconsider before committing to this design. A simple pre/post knowledge test is unlikely to meet the expectations of a graduate-level EBP project. Search the literature first to see what types of interventions have demonstrated measurable clinical outcomes for your specific problem. Your intervention must come from what the evidence recommends, not from what is easiest to measure. Discuss with your faculty or project chair before finalizing your topic.

Literature Awareness

You are not expected to have completed a literature search yet. However, you must understand: the literature must guide your intervention. You should not commit to a specific intervention until you have found evidence supporting it.

This is not optional. An evidence-based practice project without a strong supporting body of literature is not an EBP project. Your intervention will need to be grounded in what the evidence actually recommends for your specific problem and population. Do not introduce a second intervention as your comparison. Comparing two new interventions (Intervention A vs. Intervention B) changes your project into a comparative effectiveness study, which requires a higher level of IRB review and a much more complex design than is appropriate for this course.
